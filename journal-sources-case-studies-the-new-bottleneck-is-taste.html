The New Bottleneck Isn’t Code—It’s Taste

What happens when building the interface becomes easier than deciding what deserves emphasis?

By Guy Teichman·Published July 27, 2026

Make my app look man-made

Inspect the original source

The case

The app already worked.

A solo builder used Base44 to create a functional application organized around a running or walking territory concept. Basic construction was not the problem brought to the community. The builder was asking how to make the interface look better and feel less AI-made.

That distinction matters. The application had crossed the threshold from idea to working software. What remained unresolved was harder to describe as a missing feature. The builder was reacting to how the interface presented itself: not whether it functioned, but whether its decisions felt considered.

The source does not provide product metrics, user research, technical architecture, or a complete design history. It gives us a narrower case: functional output existed, and visual coherence remained in question.

What remained unresolved

Functionality does not decide what deserves attention.

A functioning interface can contain every required control and still lack a clear hierarchy. It can offer useful options while making them compete. It can apply consistent components without deciding which information should arrive first or which action should dominate.

Those choices require constraints. Someone has to decide what becomes prominent, what becomes quiet, how much information appears at once, where the eye moves next, and which elements should disappear. Interaction rhythm also matters: not merely whether a control responds, but whether each response prepares the user for the next decision.

This is the point where the article moves from report to interpretation. The source documents a builder’s concern and a set of community responses. The larger language of judgment and editorial hierarchy is our bounded reading of that material.

What the response emphasized

Study decisions, not just finishes.

The highlighted feedback did not frame the solution as a better palette or a more fashionable typeface. It pointed toward studying strong products and understanding layout, hierarchy, constraints, and interaction design.

Colors, fonts, and effects can change how a surface feels. They cannot decide what the page is asking the user to do. They do not resolve unnecessary competition between options, establish how much information belongs on screen, or determine what should be removed.

The source does not establish unanimous agreement across the community. It does, however, preserve a useful practitioner distinction: cosmetic substitution is not the same as design judgment.

Cosmetic change versus judgment

“Taste” is accumulated comparison and restraint.

Taste can sound mystical, as though some people simply possess an unexplained ability to make good interfaces. That interpretation is not useful here. In this case, taste is better understood as accumulated judgment: having compared enough products to recognize hierarchy, knowing when a constraint creates coherence, and understanding how domain expectations shape an interaction.

Cosmetic adjustment changes the surface. Design judgment decides which element earns attention, where the user should move next, which alternatives compete unnecessarily, and what can be removed without losing meaning. Restraint is not a lack of ideas. It is the ability to decide which ideas the interface can support at the same time.

That kind of judgment can be studied and practiced. It comes from comparison, revision, domain familiarity, observation, and the willingness to quiet work that is individually attractive but collectively distracting.

Evidence boundary

What we saw, what we infer, and what remains open.

Directly observed

  • A functional AI-assisted application existed.
  • The builder felt the interface looked AI-made.
  • Feedback emphasized hierarchy, layout, constraints, interaction design, and studying strong products.
  • Cosmetic substitutions were treated as insufficient.

Inferred

  • Implementation capacity may be becoming easier to acquire than design judgment.
  • Coherent decisions may become more differentiating as basic construction becomes cheaper.
  • “AI-made” may sometimes describe unresolved hierarchy and default patterns rather than AI use itself.

Not established

  • That taste is always the main bottleneck.
  • That AI assistance caused every visible issue.
  • That human-built interfaces are automatically stronger.
  • That this case represents the software market.
  • That different tools or prompts could not improve the result.

What the case suggests

Construction time can shrink before judgment time does.

AI-assisted tools may compress the time required to produce a functioning interface. They do not automatically compress the time required to build judgment. A builder still has to recognize hierarchy, sequence information, set constraints, understand the domain, and decide what not to show.

As more people can assemble working interfaces, differences in restraint, emphasis, sequencing, domain knowledge, and interaction judgment may become easier to see. The bottleneck does not necessarily disappear. It may move.

This case points toward that possibility. Its moderate evidence does not justify stronger language.

Claim boundary

What this case does not prove

One community discussion cannot establish an industry trend. Base44 or another platform may have particular defaults. Prompting quality, templates, product constraints, and the builder’s experience may all have influenced the result.

“Taste” may also be too broad. Later evidence may require separating it into hierarchy, restraint, interaction judgment, comparison, and domain familiarity. More independent cases are necessary before this principle could be considered for the Editorial Canon.

PasteLint editorial takeaway

Product design is also the editing of attention.

PasteLint does not prove the broader inference in this case. The source does help explain why the project treats design as editorial judgment rather than a search for human-looking decoration.

Typography comes before decoration. Hierarchy comes before visual effects. Each section should have one primary action. Transformation remains the visual focus. Important elements gain strength because competing elements are quieter, and the tools are organized as one editorial workflow rather than a collection of novel interface ideas.

These are not techniques for disguising generated implementation. They are decisions about what deserves emphasis and what should disappear. The practical lesson is not to make an interface look handmade. It is to make each visible choice defensible.

What evidence should come next

Keep the question open.

Useful next evidence would include similar accounts from other AI-assisted builders, before-and-after redesigns, practitioner reports from experienced product designers, and research into interface sameness or template convergence.

We would also need counterexamples: cases where AI-assisted design produced strong hierarchy, and evidence that separates tool defaults from prompting quality and user judgment. Those examples could narrow, strengthen, or overturn the interpretation offered here.

For now, the case supports a restrained conclusion: AI can accelerate construction, while the work of deciding what deserves attention may remain stubbornly human, learned, and unfinished.
