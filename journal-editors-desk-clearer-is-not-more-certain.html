Clearer Is Not More Certain

Before deleting a hedge, ask what the sentence would claim without it.

By PasteLint Editorial·Published July 27, 2026

Editorial premise

A sentence can become easier to read and harder to trust.

Some qualifiers are clutter. Others mark the boundary between what a writer knows, expects, recommends, permits, or believes.

The editorial problem

Confidence is not automatically clarity.

Editors are often taught to remove words such as may, probably, and I think. Sometimes that helps. The phrase is only a warm-up, and the useful sentence is waiting underneath.

But sometimes those words identify exactly how certain the writer is, who is making the judgment, or whether an action is optional, recommended, or required. Removing them can make the sentence cleaner while making the claim less accurate.

The editor’s job is not to defend every hedge. It is to tell disposable framing from meaningful qualification.

The words editors are taught to cut

Similar-looking phrases can do different work.

May, probably, I think, it seems, should, might, and perhaps often get grouped under labels such as hedging, hesitation, weak language, or filler.

Yet one may mark uncertainty, another permission, another capability, and another personal judgment. A qualifier can locate responsibility, express a recommendation, describe risk, or show the limits of the evidence.

A word is not filler merely because the sentence becomes shorter without it.

Possibility is not capability

Small substitutions can change the kind of claim.

Original: The event may be canceled if severe weather continues.

Changed: The event can be canceled if severe weather continues.

May describes a possible outcome here. Can may suggest ability, authority, or permission. The sentences are nearly the same length, but they do not say the same thing.

The implementation history behind this distinction is documented in Directness Without False Certainty.

Probability is not certainty

A firmer voice can exceed the evidence.

Original: The launch will probably be delayed if approval arrives after Tuesday.

Changed: The launch will be delayed if approval arrives after Tuesday.

The condition remains in both sentences. The confidence level does not. Probably describes a likely delay; removing it presents the delay as definite.

A more confident sentence is not necessarily a more accurate sentence.

Judgment is not established fact

“I think” can be part of the meaning.

Original: I think the revised menu is ready for review.

Changed: The revised menu is ready for review.

The first sentence identifies readiness as someone’s assessment. The second presents it as established. In workplace, technical, policy, and editorial writing, attribution can matter. Removing the speaker may also remove responsibility.

An editor should not turn a judgment into a fact merely because the fact sounds cleaner. That does not mean I think must always remain. It can go when the surrounding context shows that it is empty framing and the claim stays equivalent.

Negation raises the stakes

Two small words can change the situation.

Original: The library may not approve the revised menu.

Changed: The library cannot approve the revised menu.

May not approve leaves the outcome uncertain. Cannot approve suggests inability, lack of authority, or prohibition. The edit changes the whole situation, not merely its tone. Broad replacement is especially risky when negation is involved.

A suggestion is not an instruction

Shortening can change who owes what.

Suggestion: It may be helpful to review the event dates before recording.

Instruction: Review the event dates before recording.

The verb is similar, but the force changes. The first offers a suggestion; the second gives an instruction.

Original: The main point is that we should review the dates before recording.

Safer revision: We should review the dates before recording.

The announcement frame can go. We, should, and before recording remain. Revision should preserve who is acting and how strongly the action is proposed.

Change the frame, not the claim

Directness can come from making the action easier to find.

Original: We were hoping you could confirm whether the library may not approve the menu.

Direct revision: Please confirm whether the library may not approve the menu.

The conversational opening changes. The requested action moves forward. The words may not survive, so the question inside the request remains intact.

Change the frame. Preserve the claim.

Make the action easier to locate without making the writer more certain.

Why revision flattens distinctions

Style advice becomes risky when it operates mechanically.

Any revision process—human or automated—can overvalue brevity, confidence, active voice, directness, or visible change. A process rewarded for producing a firmer version may remove words without determining what those words contribute.

A human editor can make the same mistake by applying a useful guideline in every context. The problem begins when a style preference is treated as permission to change the claim.

What SecondDraft changed

A bounded revision tool should know when to stop.

SecondDraft stopped globally deleting or replacing several qualified expressions. In tested contexts, it preserves uncertainty, recommendation strength, actors, conditions, timing, and protected values. It can still shorten supported request frames, and sometimes it deliberately leaves a sentence unchanged.

PasteLint Clean handles deterministic mechanical cleanup before editorial revision. The Text Readiness Framework places both steps in the larger Paste → Clean → Revise → Prepare workflow.

Under the hood

SecondDraft is browser-only and rule-based. Its preservation is bounded by tested examples and patterns. It does not fact-check or understand arbitrary language as a human editor would.

Sometimes the right edit is no edit

Restraint is part of editorial judgment.

Editing quality is not measured by edit count. Leaving a meaningful qualifier intact is not timidity; it can be the only defensible choice. A revision may improve the framing while refusing to increase certainty.

The goal is not to make every sentence firmer. The goal is to make every change defensible.

What this principle does not mean

Not every hedge is useful.

Some phrases are empty framing. Some writers bury clear actions under unnecessary politeness. Repetition and clutter can still be revised. Context determines whether a qualifier carries meaning.

SecondDraft does not independently verify facts. High-stakes legal, policy, contractual, medical, or financial text still requires human review.

Editorial takeaway

Clearer is not more certain.

Before deleting a qualifier, determine what it is doing. It may be weakening the sentence. It may also mark the exact boundary between what the writer knows, expects, recommends, permits, or believes.
